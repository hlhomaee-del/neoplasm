What is a Neoplasm?

Breaking Down the Word

Neo = New   |   Plasm = Growth / Formation

A neoplasm is an abnormal new growth of cells. The cells grow and divide more than they should, or don't die when they normally would.

⚠️ Key exam concept: Not every neoplasm is cancer. Not every tumor is malignant. "Neoplasm" is the broadest term — it includes benign, malignant, and everything in between.

✅ Section Complete!

Breaking Down the Words

Why This Matters

On the CBCS exam, the medical term often contains clues about the type of neoplasm. Knowing word parts helps you quickly narrow down whether a condition is benign or malignant, and what tissue is involved.

Key Suffixes to Know

-oma = tumor (usually benign, but NOT always!)

-carcinoma = malignant tumor of epithelial tissue

-sarcoma = malignant tumor of connective tissue

⚠️ Exam trap: Melanoma, lymphoma, and hepatoma end in "-oma" but are malignant. Don't assume "-oma" always means benign!

Benign vs Malignant vs In Situ

✅ Benign

Does NOT invade surrounding tissue. Does NOT spread. Stays localized. Usually not life-threatening. Example: uterine leiomyoma (fibroid).

🔴 Malignant

Invades surrounding tissue. Can spread (metastasize) to other parts of the body. This is what we call cancer. Example: lung carcinoma.

🟡 In Situ

Abnormal cells are present but have NOT invaded surrounding tissue. They are "in place." Think of it as pre-invasive or stage 0. Example: carcinoma in situ of cervix.

❓ Uncertain vs Unspecified Behavior

Uncertain: The pathologist or provider states the behavior cannot be determined — "uncertain whether benign or malignant." The nature is genuinely unclear even after evaluation.

Unspecified: The documentation simply does not state the behavior. No information about benign or malignant. The provider didn't specify.

⚠️ Exam trap: These are NOT the same. Uncertain = clinically unclear. Unspecified = not documented.

Primary vs Secondary Site

Understanding the Difference

Primary site: Where the cancer originated. This is the organ or tissue where the neoplasm first started.

Secondary site: Where the cancer spread to. Also called a metastatic site.

Example: A patient has colon cancer that has spread to the liver. The colon is the primary site. The liver is the secondary (metastatic) site.

⚠️ Critical Exam Traps

1. If the provider documents "metastatic cancer to the liver," the liver is the secondary site — NOT the primary.

2. If the primary site is unknown, you still code the secondary site as secondary. Do NOT assume a primary site.

3. "Metastatic from" = primary site. "Metastatic to" = secondary site.

4. When cancer has spread, you may need to code BOTH the primary and the secondary site.

✅ Section Complete!

You now understand how to identify primary and secondary sites and recognize the critical difference between "metastatic from" and "metastatic to."

How to Use the Neoplasm Table

The Neoplasm Table is Your Best Friend

The ICD-10-CM Neoplasm Table in the Alphabetic Index is the key lookup tool. It lists anatomical sites (body locations) down the left side and behavior types across the top as columns.

Here are the steps to use it:

Step 1: Identify the anatomical site from the documentation.

Step 2: Determine the behavior (malignant primary, malignant secondary, in situ, benign, uncertain, unspecified).

Step 3: Find the site in the table — it's listed alphabetically. Look for the main entry, then find any indented sub-entries.

Step 4: Move across to the correct column for the behavior.

Step 5: Write down the code, then verify it in the Tabular List for accuracy and any additional instructions.

How to Read Documentation for Coding

On the exam, you'll read clinical scenarios and determine the correct code. Here's your process:

1. Read the entire note. 2. Identify the diagnosis or reason for the encounter. 3. Pick out the site. 4. Pick out the behavior. 5. Determine if it's primary or secondary. 6. Use the neoplasm table. 7. Verify in the Tabular List.
